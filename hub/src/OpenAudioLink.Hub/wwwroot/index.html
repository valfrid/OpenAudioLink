Cast points

A named place to send audio. A room and a group are the same thing — "Kitchen" holds one speaker, "House" holds all of them, and a producer sends one identical stream to however many are in it. This page is for setup: naming rooms and choosing which speakers are in them. Each cast point is also offered to Spotify under its own name, so once the rooms exist, choosing one happens from the phone and nobody opens this page to listen.

Node-to-node link test

A producer node streams a synthetic source at the real profile — L24 stereo, 48 kHz, 5 ms packets, 200 per second — straight to the consumers you pick. The Hub only says who sends to whom; the audio never passes through it.
Pattern is derived from each frame's position in the stream, so the consumer recomputes every sample and reports any that differ. That catches corruption a sequence-number check cannot see. Tone is a sine — worth listening to once a DAC exists, useless for verification, so payload errors are expected with it. The third option sends what the producer is actually capturing — its ADC on a turntable node, its MEMS capsule on a microphone node, whichever its input setting selects. The option names whichever one the chosen producer has. It is the real analog path and the only one of the three that leaves the board; payload errors are meaningless with it too.
Microphone into a speaker in the same room is a feedback loop. At this buffer it returns as a slap-back rather than a squeal, which makes it easy to mistake for working — start with the volume low or the two in different rooms.
Payload errors accumulate until Stop & reset counters, so a Pattern run started after a Tone run inherits the tone’s. Reset first if the number is going to be read.

Hub streaming

Streams audio as RTP to one or more receivers, or to a computer running a player such as GStreamer or ffplay. Select several destinations with Ctrl+click, or list addresses separated by commas. One stream runs at a time. The test tone is a diagnostic — it answers "is this speaker working?" without involving a source.
The measurement sweep is the other half of a room measurement: 20 Hz to 20 kHz over eight seconds, then two seconds of silence for the room to stop ringing, repeating. Record it back through a microphone node and dividing one by the other gives the room's frequency response. Measure one loudspeaker at a time. Two playing the same sweep arrive at the microphone at different times, and where they cancel belongs to the pair rather than to either speaker — so the channel chooses which one answers, and nodes set to the other half stay silent.

Room measurement

What the room did to the sweep: a recording divided by the signal that made it. This runs the whole thing — sweep to one speaker, microphone recorded to a file, both stopped after the sweeps you asked for, then the curve. The sweep and the recorder are still on their own panels above, and nothing here stops you driving them by hand; this is the order between them, which is where the mistakes were.
One speaker at a time. Two playing the same sweep arrive at the microphone at different times, and where they cancel belongs to the pair rather than to either one — a correction fitted to that makes both of them worse. Put the microphone where you listen, not beside the speaker.
The curve is levelled so that 200 Hz–2 kHz sits at 0 dB, because the shape is the measurement and the absolute level would be a property of the microphone's sensitivity, which is not calibrated. A sixth of an octave is averaged into each point: a raw response is a forest of interference nulls that move when the microphone moves an inch, and what survives the smoothing is what belongs to the room.
Read the warnings before the curve. A clipped recording measures the clipping, and it looks exactly like a real measurement.
Six sweeps takes eighty seconds: two more cycles are recorded than are averaged, because the recording starts at an arbitrary point in one and the first complete one is discarded while the receiver is still filling its buffer.

Pick the speaker under test, the node with the microphone, and press Measure. It runs the sweep, records, stops both and draws the curve.

Tick the measurements to draw. Two on one chart is the whole reason they are kept: left against right says which speaker, before against after says whether a correction worked, and the same speaker with the microphone moved says whether a peak belongs to the room or to where you were sitting. Name them so they can be told apart later.

Writing sends both vectors, the shared headroom and the switch in one request, so a speaker is never left running one channel's correction against the other's. The headroom is the deeper of the two channels' needs: it is a broadband cut, so different values left and right would walk the stereo image sideways.
Then measure again with the correction on, name it "after", and put the two curves on the chart. That is the only thing that says whether it worked. The on/off switch lives beside each speaker in the device table, next to the vectors it applies.

Firmware

Version and build time are read out of the image itself, so you can see what an upload actually contains — an update that reinstalls the version already running otherwise looks exactly like a successful one.
Fetching is not installing. It downloads the published image to this Hub; pressing Update on a node stays a separate, deliberate act. Automatically flashing every speaker in a house is a way to lose an evening.

Diagnostics log

One row per speaker every 30 seconds — buffer, trims, pads, step-backs, loss, stalls, jitter, signal, Wi-Fi drops, clock, and the Hub's own send counters on the same row. Written whether or not this page is open, and costing the speakers nothing: it records readings the Hub already takes.
Download the file rather than photographing the panel. A screenshot is one instant, and the question that settles an argument here is whether a counter moved when something happened. Rows answer that; a picture of the current values cannot.
